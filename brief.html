Training environments for chip-verification agents

We build tasks from real Verilog designs where a simulator, not a judge model, decides the score. Our newest task is the core of a verification engineer's job: read the spec and write the tests that catch the bugs. Current frontier models are far from solving it.

The result

Writing a correct test suite from a spec is still hard for frontier models

The task. The model gets the specification and the module's port list. It never sees an implementation. It writes a cocotb test suite. We run that suite on the correct design and on 40 hidden buggy versions per design.

Two scores. Strict is the share of buggy versions the suite catches, and it is 0 if any check fails on the correct design, the way a real suite with a false alarm is unusable. Partial drops the checks that fail on the correct design and counts what the rest catch. It measures coverage.

The gap between models is real. With 104 suites per model, the 95% confidence interval for Terra's strict score is 66% to 82% and for Luna's is 8% to 20%. The task separates models. On the two hardest designs we also ran GPT-6 Astra (5 suites each): 70% on the UART and 54% on the I2C master, far ahead of Terra but still short of solving them.

The UART is hard for everyone. Only 2 of Terra's 13 UART suites passed the correct design. Its coverage is good (69%), but nearly every suite carried at least one wrong check. The I2C master is next: 7 of Terra's 13 suites failed the correct design, 6 of them on the same one-clock detail. The SPI controller is the other extreme: every Terra suite passed and caught every hidden bug, so it now separates weaker models only.

Small designs are not counted. On our 10 small designs, suites that ran caught nearly every bug. They are too easy to say anything about frontier models.

Cost. About 3 to 4 cents per Terra suite at list price. Grading runs locally and is free.

Where the models fail

Real verification mistakes, not trick questions

Losing track of state

Forgetting that interrupt flags stay latched after reset, that a claimed interrupt stays in service, or that the test itself disabled a source earlier.

Misreading the register map

Treating the one-shot bit as periodic, reading "RX FIFO full" as "TX FIFO full", dropping a shift from the ID formula, testing a read/write register as read-only.

Testbench mechanics

Sampling a signal before it settles, missing a waveform edge, starting four clock drivers on one pin, reading a signal that does not exist.

Consequences of the spec

On the UART, turning on the glitch filter starts a phantom frame, and lowering the baud prescaler below the running counter stops all ticks. On the I2C master, the transfer-in-progress flag clears one clock after the done flag rises, and many suites checked it in that clock. All follow from the spec; none is spelled out.

Every failure checked

We proved each failure was the model's fault

A failure only means something if our spec and our reference design are right. So for every suite on the eight realistic designs that failed on the correct design, we found each failing check, worked out the correct value from the spec text alone, put that value into the test, and reran the test to the end in simulation. Where the cause was timing, we read the reference design's internal state to confirm it followed the spec rule cycle by cycle.

127failing tests, each traced to a specific model mistake and a specific line of the spec, then rerun to the end

We had found 12 more gaps in earlier rounds, on the UART (11) and timer (1). On our hardest design, many failures that looked like "the model can't do this" were really "our spec didn't say this." We fixed those before measuring. Most dataset builders never find them, and they end up in training data.

Who it's for

Teams improving the model behind a chip agent

Frontier labs

Hard, verifiable hardware tasks to train models with reinforcement learning, with a score that comes from simulation and a continuous reward (bugs caught) for verification work.

Chip-AI companies

Evaluations to measure their agents and training data to improve them, without hand-building hundreds of verified tasks and clean specs.

EDA vendors

Benchmarks and training sets for AI features in verification tools, on designs they can license and audit.

How a task is made

Start from a design that works, break it, keep only breaks we can catch

step 1

A working design

A correct Verilog design with a written spec. 11 are written in-house; 7 are open-source IP (three efabless cores, Apache-2.0, and four OpenCores cores: I2C, SPI, AES and a floating-point unit), credited.

step 2

Break it automatically

Our injector makes realistic edits: a flipped comparison, a wrong constant, a swapped signal, a dropped condition, a register read one cycle early.

step 3

Keep only catchable bugs

Each broken version is simulated. If our checks can't tell it from the original, it is thrown away, so every bug is real and detectable.

step 4

Grade by simulation

A fix must pass hidden checks, including a side-by-side run against the original over thousands of random cycles. A test suite must pass the original and fail the broken versions.

real task · timer IP · one injected bug and the hidden check's verdict

The model never sees the original design, the hidden tests, the hidden buggy versions, or the random inputs the grader uses.

Bug-fix tasks

Fixing a planted bug is close to solved; testing is not

We started with "find and fix the bug" tasks. Current models now solve most of them in one answer: GPT-6 Luna fixes 92.6% of our 1,009 bug-fix and fill-in tasks one-shot, and 95.7% of a set built around deliberately subtle bugs. When the specification is precise, a careful model can compare every line against it.

The tasks still work as agent environments. Earlier, GPT-5.6 Luna went from 9 to 30 of 40 hard tasks when it could read, edit, compile and run tests in our sandbox, with no attempts to game the grade. But the difficulty that matters now is in verification: deciding what to test and getting every expected value right.

For reference: on NVIDIA's CVDP benchmark (non-agentic, problems that need no commercial simulator), GPT-6 Luna scores 56.0% and GPT-5.6 Luna 50.7%.

What we haven't shown yet

Open questions, stated plainly

  • More hard designs. The verification results rest on 8 realistic designs. Two of them (GPIO, SPI) no longer challenge Terra; the hardest are the UART and I2C master. We are adding more of similar size.
  • Training transfer. We have not yet trained a model on these tasks and measured it on an outside benchmark. That is the next proof point.
  • Reward design. The strict score is the honest measure but gives sparse reward. The partial score rewards coverage but, used alone as a reward, would not punish wrong checks. A training reward needs both.
  • Top model. GPT-6 Astra has run only on the two hardest designs, 5 suites each, so its numbers are rough.
